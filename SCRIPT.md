# CareerCloset Final Fundraising Pitch

**Format:** 8:00 pitch, 3:00 investor questions, 2:00 coaching. Slide timings apply to the pitch only. The Q&A slide follows the close.

## Eight-minute pitch

### 0:00–0:25 | Slide 1
CareerCloset makes professional clothing more accessible to students who need it for interviews, career fairs, and presentations. Instead of buying an outfit for a few events each semester, a student can reserve a curated rental near campus. We are asking for $15,000 to prove one campus can run a paid, repeatable pilot.

### 0:25–1:10 | Slide 2
The customer problem we are testing is straightforward: career opportunities have a dress code, but students do not all have the same access to professional clothing. We need to be precise about what we know. This package contains no completed CareerCloset interviews, paid rentals, or measured demand. We are not claiming traction. The first evidence target is 30 interviews about event frequency, current alternatives, fit needs, and willingness to pay.

### 1:10–1:50 | Slide 3
The product loop is deliberately simple. A student selects an event and size, reserves an outfit online, picks it up during a campus window, and returns it after the event. We start with curated inventory rather than building a large wardrobe. We will document condition at pickup and track cleaning and returns. Deposits or card authorization are proposed controls to test, not proven safeguards.

### 1:50–2:25 | Slide 4
Our beachhead is one campus, with an assumed 10,000 students, and one trusted distribution partner such as a career center. Student organizations and employers could extend reach, but we do not have confirmed partners. The pilot must show which sizes turn, whether the fit works, what students will pay, and whether returns are reliable. That is the evidence that determines whether this is a business, not just a useful idea.

### 2:25–3:15 | Slide 5
The rental model has a testable unit-economic hypothesis. We assume a $29 rental and $8 for cleaning and handling, leaving $21 contribution before fixed costs. A $79 semester membership and campus or employer sponsorship are other possible revenue streams, but neither is validated or committed. We will record price acceptance, actual cleaning cost, damage, inventory utilization, and repeat use. If those change the contribution, the model changes with them.

### 3:15–4:05 | Slide 6
The ask is $15,000 for a six-month validation round. The proposed instrument is a SAFE with a $135,000 post-money valuation cap. The simple illustrative ownership at the cap is $15,000 divided by $150,000, or 10 percent. This is a classroom assumption, not an independent valuation, signed term sheet, or legal recommendation. We chose a bounded ask because both demand and inventory utilization are unproven.

### 4:05–4:55 | Slide 7
The use of funds is tied to four jobs. $6,000, or 40 percent, buys curated initial inventory. $2,000, or about 13 percent, establishes cleaning and alteration operations. $4,000, or about 27 percent, funds the campus pilot and acquisition. The remaining $3,000, or 20 percent, covers software, support, and contingency. Those allocations total exactly $15,000. They are a proposed budget, not expenditures already made.

### 4:55–5:45 | Slide 8
We assume zero cash before financing and average net burn of $2,500 per month. The runway calculation is $15,000 divided by $2,500, which equals six months. Under an even-burn scenario, cash falls from $12,500 after month one to $2,500 after month five and zero after month six. This is a planning scenario, not a forecast supported by operating history. We would monitor cash monthly and pause discretionary inventory purchases if actual burn runs ahead.

### 5:45–6:40 | Slide 9
At month six, the target is 30 interviews, 75 completed pilot rentals, one paid campus or sponsor, and at least 80 percent on-time returns. We also require positive rental contribution and at least 85 percent fit satisfaction before expanding. We will measure on-time returns as on-time returned rentals divided by rentals due, and fit satisfaction from post-rental responses. If a gate fails, we pause inventory growth and fix or stop the model rather than spending through the evidence.

### 6:40–7:25 | Slide 10
The risks are fit gaps, low utilization, damage, late returns, and cleaning cost. Each can reduce the assumed $21 contribution. The proposed controls are short rental windows, condition records, deposits or card authorization, and return reminders. We prefer this small SAFE to debt before cash flow is predictable, and to a larger equity round before the hypothesis is proven. The investor is funding a bounded test with explicit release gates.

### 7:25–8:00 | Slide 11
Our founder objective for this round is affordable professional access, earned through disciplined proof: fund one campus, measure every rental, and expand only if both access and unit economics work. We are asking for $15,000 to turn a customer hypothesis into a paid, measurable campus pilot. Thank you. We welcome your questions.

## Investor questions | 3:00

Keep answers direct: state what is assumed, how the pilot will measure it, then name the decision gate. Do not convert a target into a result.

**What stops damage or non-returns?** We do not know the loss rate yet. The pilot will test deposits or card authorization, documented condition, short rental windows, reminders, and a clear late-return policy. We will track the on-time return rate and actual replacement/cleaning cost. Expansion requires at least 80 percent on-time returns and positive contribution; otherwise we pause inventory growth.

**How do you know students will pay $29?** We do not know yet. The $29 price is an assumption. Thirty interviews will test current alternatives and willingness to pay, and completed rentals will show actual conversion and repeat behavior. We will revise the price and contribution calculation using observed data.

**Why only one campus, and what does success unlock?** One campus limits inventory and operating complexity while we learn fit, utilization, and return behavior. Success is not just 75 rentals: we also need one paid campus or sponsor, positive contribution, at least 85 percent fit satisfaction, and the return target. Only then would we test a second campus.

**What if the $2,500 monthly burn is wrong?** It is a planning assumption. We will update a monthly cash schedule from actual spending and collections. If burn is higher, the six-month plan shortens; we would reduce acquisition or inventory spending and protect the milestone data before requesting more capital.

## Coaching | 2:00

**0:00–0:20 | Listen.** Do not interrupt or defend the deck. Capture the coach’s main point and any specific suggested change.

**0:20–1:10 | Reflect.** Restate the advice in one sentence and ask whether the team understood it correctly. Separate the observation from the proposed solution.

**1:10–1:40 | Decide.** Say whether the team will adopt, test, or decline the advice, with a reason tied to customer evidence, economics, or founder objectives.

**1:40–2:00 | Commit.** Name the concrete change, owner, and next check. Add the advice and resulting change to the group revision log after the session.
